# Decisions made without you

One line each, with the reason. Newest at the bottom.

- Boost/arcade level goes after item 7 and before item 8: it was added after the list was written, and this keeps your order intact.
- Eight drivers, with two free and six costing 300 to 1,200 coins: enough variety without the price competing with routes.
- Drivers are cosmetic only: a perk would make a coin purchase affect the fairness of the score.
- In the cockpit the driver is shown by their hands on the wheel and their face in a new interior mirror: the body has to stay hidden or it blocks the view.
- Added a sixth menu tab, Drivers, and renamed Driver to Career: selection and career stats are different jobs.
- Added tools/gate.sh so every item is checked the same way before it is committed.
- Pivot to Colombia received mid-night. Stopped before starting liveries, tagged `milestone-pre-colombia-pivot`, and replaced the remaining backlog as you asked.
- Boost/arcade level is off tonight's plan: your revised backlog replaces the remaining items and does not list it. It is not rejected, so ask if you want it back.
- Old map expansion is off the plan: the Colombian city rework replaces it.
- The city is called Puerto Marisol, from "mar y sol": it sounds Caribbean and no real Colombian city has that name as far as I know.
- Money keeps its internal unit, and each pack sets how much one is worth. In Colombia one coin shows as $100, so a route at 500 reads $50.000 and a fare can be a real-looking $2.900.
- Packs live in `data/packs/<country>`, and the left-hand test pack lives in `test/fixtures` so it never ships.
- Colombia keeps all six languages until item 6, so the game stays playable in the meantime.
- Did the file part of the language item early: it saved translating every new string into four languages that were about to leave the game. French, Portuguese, Hindi and Arabic are kept in `data/lang/archive` and their fonts stay in the repo, but neither ships in the APK.
- Sea to the east, with the old river road becoming the Malecón: it was already the edge of town and gave the city a coast without moving a road.
- Speed bumps went from 2 to 4 for the Colombian feel, keeping the warnings you asked for. Potholes went from 4 to 14.
- Street stalls can be knocked over and cost points, but no vendor stands at them: a person flying through the air is not the warmth you asked for.
- A hit motorbike stays upright and the rider just stops. It counts as a collision, with nothing shown that could be read as an injury.
- Cars include many yellow taxis, as in Colombian cities.
- Fictional makers are Caimán and Alcatraz, and the rapid-transit line is La Ola. None of these is a real bus brand or operator as far as I know.
- The old City 12 and Midi 10 stay as the Caimán Busetón 12 and Caimán B10, so that work and any saves that own them carry over.
- The chiva needs driver level 5 as well as $400.000, to make it feel like a special unlock. Catalog items can now set a `level`.
- The articulated bus comes with its route, not from the garage: it only makes sense on the busway.
- A chiva has an open step instead of doors, so the door button opens and closes nothing visible but still runs the stop.
- All four buseta routes became informal: people flag the bus down at random spots each shift, on top of the named stops. Bays stay in the code for later countries, and the left-hand test pack still uses them.
- On an informal route only the end time is really timed. Each hail gets a share of it by distance, so the on-time display still works.
- Driving past someone flagging the bus is only a small loss. Driving past someone who asked to get off costs more, and they ride on to the next stop.
- A fare is $2.900 on a buseta and $3.100 on La Ola. The driver's pay stays a small share of that, about $300 a passenger, so the economy did not need rebalancing.
- A vendor boards after everyone else, works the aisle for two stops and gets off, and gives a small boost to satisfaction.
- La Ola is a closed busway that loops round the south and east of the city, so it crosses no other road. It turns back round an island at Portal del Mar.
- Passengers on La Ola pay at the station, so there is no stop at the ticket machine.
- People running for the bus and the wheelchair ramp were on the old list but not your revised one, so I left them out.
- Ten drivers instead of eight, five women and five men from their twenties to their sixties. It is a Caribbean depot: eight are costeños, most from Puerto Marisol itself, and Rubén Cardona from Medellín and Blanca Sanabria from Tunja are the two from the interior. Skin tone does not follow region: the paisa is brown-skinned, Daniela from Barranquilla is light, and the deep-skinned Esteban grew up on the Malecón.
- The free drivers are Wilmer Pertuz and Yuleidis Simarra, both from Puerto Marisol, with Wilmer as the default: a man and a woman from the city where the game is set.
- Prices still run from 300 to 1,200 coins, and no region or skin tone sits at one end of the list, so the cheapest drivers never look like the "basic" ones.
- Driver names pair common first names with regional surnames such as Mestra from Córdoba and Uriana, a Wayuu clan name. Every full name was searched on the web and matches nobody notable. The Medellín driver was Hernán Zuluaga until review found that name belongs to a real man in crime news linked to Medellín, so he is now Rubén Cardona. Blanca Avella became Blanca Sanabria, as Avella reads as the senator Aída Avella.
- Yolima Uriana, the Wayuu-heritage driver, dresses like the others. Her bio leads with wanting to drive La Ola one day, and then says she talks to her grandmother in Wayuunaiki every Sunday. No face paint or traditional dress: those carry cultural meaning and would read as a costume on a bus driver.
- Bios are about the person rather than where they come from: family, football, coffee, Carnival, studying, saving for a buseta. Traits are spread across skin tones: Esteban, who is Black, studies engineering at night and wears glasses, Wilmer plays beach football, and Fanny's bio leads with her authority as the trainer. The Spanish uses Colombian words like tinto, mijo, izada de bandera, devolverse and patio for the depot.
- A headwrap is a hat, not a hair style, so it covers whatever hair is under it, in the portrait and the 3D figures alike.
- A catalog entry with the old "cap": true still wears a cap, so older packs keep working.
- Drivers wear their own short-sleeved shirts, a polo or a guayabera, with bare forearms at the wheel. A jacket-style shirt and tie is not what anyone drives a buseta in at 32°C. Each driver has their own shirt colour, and Wilmer keeps a towel over his shoulder.
- The cap is a baseball cap with its peak in front. The old peaked cap with a gold band read as police or security.
- A catalog entry with the old "outfit": "uniform" now wears a polo.
- The mirror shows only what its glass would. LowPolyBuilder can now trim any shape to a window, so a vueltiao or long braids stop at the frame instead of hanging below it.
- The mirror face is squashed flat, as a reflection is. A face with depth peeped past the frame in taller buses and in left-hand packs, because the eye sits at a different angle to the glass there. Its side flips with the doors.
- The mirror face is unshaded and slightly dimmed. A reflection shows the face as the windscreen lights it, and in the cab's shade deep skin tones lost their eyes.
- An old save with a driver who has left falls back to the default driver. The bought ids stay in the save, where they do nothing, and nobody is refunded because those drivers never left this branch.
- Passengers are 20 looks and two vendors in passengers.json. The skins, hairs and tops palettes are gone, because seated riders and pavement walkers now take their colours from the same looks.
- Children, adults and older people each come in deep, medium and light skin tones, and so do people in work clothes. Tests keep it that way, so no role or age belongs to one skin tone.
- The nurse is a man and the football fan is a young woman, to avoid the usual defaults.
- The football fan wears a plain yellow shirt with denim shorts, trainers and a vueltiao, as fans do in Barranquilla. No crest and no team, and no full kit, which made her look like a player.
- The Wayuu woman passenger wears a manta, an everyday dress in La Guajira, in one colour with a single band and nothing ceremonial.
- The market seller carries her basket at her side. A fruit bowl on the head is the tourist-postcard palenquera, and I left that image out.
- Walkers are dressed from a shuffled deck each time they step out, so a queue rarely shows the same person twice. Before, each pooled walker kept one random look forever.
- A seated rider takes the colours and size of whoever walked to that seat, and whoever gets off wears the look of the rider who stood up, so nobody changes clothes on the bus. When more are aboard than there are seats, someone standing gets off and is dressed from the deck.
- No passenger shares a driver's head: looks that matched a driver changed their hair, hat or beard. Black women and girls wear a ponytail, a bun, short natural hair or braids, and a medium-skinned schoolgirl has the braids.
- A headwrap is low and rounded, rising up and back with a fold of a second fabric and a knot at one side. The tall stacked version read as a fez or a hard hat.
- Things carried in the hand sit on the left, away from the right arm that pays and waves.
- A long skirt is one panel to the ankle with the feet stepping out under it. Two swinging panels looked like wide trousers.
- Seated riders' arms now rest on their laps. On the 2.3 m buseta, arms at the sides poked out through the wall beside a window seat.
- Pavement walkers are now three batches, for clothes, skin and hair, instead of one. The single batch tinted every face the colour of its shirt. It costs two draw calls in total, not per person.
- The vendors are two looks in the data, a man and a woman, picked at each stop. They replace the vendor mesh that was built in code.
- Drivers and passengers share HeadParts for faces, hair and hats, so a vueltiao or braids look the same on both.
- CountryPack.validate() reports a passengers file with no looks, bad colours, unknown styles or sizes out of range. The game itself falls back to one default person rather than stopping.
- Brake is a hold button instead of a slider. It starts at 45% and firms up to full over about 1.5 s, so a quick press slows gently and stays under the harsh-braking limit, while a long hold stops hard.
- The GO pedal stays analogue (press higher for more power): only the brake was asked about.
- Drive and reverse is a two-half switch, D and R, with the gear in use lit green or amber. Tapping a half picks that gear, which is easier to read than a toggle.
- Changing gear still needs the bus stopped, but a yellow tip now says "Stop the bus to change gear". Before, the button silently did nothing, which is what confused new players.
- First launch is Colombian Spanish unless the phone is in English, then English. Any other phone language gets Spanish, because the game is made for Colombia. A saved choice always wins.
- The game's name stays "City Bus Shift" on the menu in both languages. It is the app's name on the phone and the store, and a second Spanish name would make one game look like two. It is still a working title.
- Stop and street names have no translation entries. They are Spanish proper names, like any place in Colombia, so English players see "Malecón Norte" too. A test fails if one looks English, so the two languages never mix.
- Bus model names (Caimán B8, La Chiva) and driver names are names, so they are not translated. Livery names are Spanish words already and keep identity entries.
- Spanish words chosen for the coast: bus and buseta, paradero for a stop, "Toca" for taps, manejar, sardinel for the kerb, andén for the pavement, resalto for a speed bump, sacudón for a hard jolt, pito for the horn, direccional (DIR on the buttons), glorieta, pasaje, choque, frenazo, caneca for a bin, guacal for a crate, celular, timón for the steering wheel and "pilas" in tips.
- "Configuración" over "Ajustes", as Colombian phones say it. It fits the side menu button at 1280 wide.
- The Career tab is "Progreso" in Spanish. "Carrera" is also the word for a street in Colombian addresses, and the city has a Carrera Central.
- "Parada" stays only in "hacer la parada" and "pedir la parada", the everyday phrases for flagging a bus and asking to get off. Everywhere else a stop is a paradero.
- The GO pedal is ACELERAR. "ACEL" was not a word anyone uses, and "DALE" would read oddly in the tutorial.
- "Select" and "Selected" are "Usar" and "En uso", which need no gender for a bus, a paint or a driver. The welcome line avoids "Bienvenido" for the same reason.
- "Left %d waiting" is "Dejaste %d esperando", which is right for one person or many. The old wording said "se quedaron" even for one.
- Steering sensitivity shows as a percentage. "1.00" is wrong in Colombia, where the decimal mark is a comma, and a percentage needs no decimal mark in either language.
- Money keeps the dot for thousands in both languages, because it is Colombian pesos. "Carry 1,000 passengers" is "1.000" in Spanish.
- Removed five unused "coins" strings from the Spanish file. Money is shown in pesos now, so they would never appear.
- Text drawn on HUD buttons, pedals and the gauge shrinks to fit its box. "PUERTAS ABIERTAS" was cut off, and this keeps any future longer word inside its button too.
- The route panel and the penalty toast are 560 px wide instead of 480 and 400. The stop line in Spanish ran into the lateness, and there is room between the top buttons and the map even on a 16:9 phone.
- The tutorial message keeps clear of the indicator and horn buttons. On a 16:9 phone it covered part of both, so it now gets narrower and taller there.
- The garage's "Tap a bus or paint" note wraps. In Spanish it was wide enough to push the whole menu past the right edge at 1280 wide.
- The performance readout now goes through tr() like everything else, since players can turn it on in settings.
- tools/test.sh waits up to 300 s by default instead of 90 s, close to the 280 s gate.sh already passes. The suite takes about 170 s on this Mac, and the old limit killed a passing run.
- The gear is always "el cambio" ("meter el cambio"), never Spain's "marcha". The tests now ban "marcha", "recto", "retraso", "aparca", "maletero" and the "usted" and "vosotros" forms, since the game speaks as "tú".
- Tutorial and settings text about steering names no control ("Mantén el bus en el carril", "Sensibilidad de la dirección"), because tilt and button steering have no wheel on screen.
- "Pedido de parada" in the shift report counts as "pedir la parada", so it keeps "parada". A stop the driver passes is "saltado", since "perdido" first reads as "lost".
- Penalty toasts have no exclamation marks in either language ("Choque", "Frenazo"), matching the English and the other toasts.
- Lateness is a noun ("de atraso", "de adelanto") and the opening line ends "Y tú al timón", so no text gives the player a gender. The "Old Hand" achievement is "Pura experiencia" for the same reason.
- A street stall is a "puesto de venta", because "puestos" already means bus seats.
- The ad offer's English is "Watch an ad: double your earnings", since money is shown in pesos, not coins.
- The livery is "Fiesta Tricolor", in Spanish word order. Its id stays tricolor_fiesta, so saves still work.
- Stop names keep the "de" of Colombian place names: "Mercado de Oriente", "Cruce de Los Almendros", "Puente de Los Almendros". "Los Almendros" is the barrio, so it keeps its capital.
- The visible-text scan skips a line only when it starts with print, push_error and the like, and drops just the node path from a get_node call, so text on the same line is still checked.
- The penalty toast now shrinks its text to fit, like the HUD buttons. "Te saltaste el paradero Puente de Los Almendros" was a little wider than the 560 px box.
